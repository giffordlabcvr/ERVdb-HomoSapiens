# ERV-1.Gamma.E (HERV-E)

ERV-1.Gamma.E (HERV-E) is a lineage of ancient ERV1 gammaretroviruses that forms part of the classical group of human endogenous retroviruses described during the early years of HERV research. Members of the lineage entered the genomes of primate ancestors millions of years ago and subsequently accumulated numerous copies throughout the genome.

Unlike some endogenous retroviral lineages that are notable for preserved viral proteins or reconstructed viral biology, HERV-E is primarily recognised for its influence on host gene regulation. Numerous HERV-E-derived long terminal repeats (LTRs) have been co-opted as promoters, enhancers and other regulatory elements, contributing to the expression of nearby cellular genes. As a result, much of the biological significance attributed to HERV-E arises from individual loci rather than from the lineage as a whole.

HERV-E loci have been reported to be active in a variety of developmental and pathological contexts, including placental tissues, immune cells and several forms of cancer. In some cases, HERV-E-derived regulatory sequences have been shown to drive tissue-specific or disease-associated patterns of gene expression. These observations illustrate how ancient retroviral insertions can become integrated into host regulatory networks long after the loss of their original viral functions.

Historically, HERV-E has attracted considerable attention in studies of autoimmune disease and cancer, and was among the lineages investigated during early searches for replication-competent human endogenous retroviruses. However, no infectious HERV-E virus is known, and most HERV-E loci are highly degraded remnants of ancient integrations.

The HERV-E lineage provides an important example of how endogenous retroviruses can shape genome function through regulatory innovation. Although the original viruses that generated these insertions have long since disappeared, HERV-E-derived sequences continue to influence the activity of the human genome and contribute to the evolutionary history of gene regulation in primates.
