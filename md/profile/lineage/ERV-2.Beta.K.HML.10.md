# ERV-2.Beta.K.HML.10 (HERV-K-HML10)

**ERV-2.Beta.K.HML.10 (HERV-K-HML10)** is an ancient endogenous betaretrovirus lineage belonging to the **K** sublineage of betaretroviruses (*Betaretrovirus*). HML10 is a relatively small lineage, represented by approximately nine proviral loci in the human reference genome together with numerous derived solo LTRs. Comparative analyses indicate that the lineage entered primate genomes during the evolution of **Catarrhini (Old World monkeys and apes)**, with most integrations occurring approximately **20-40 million years ago**.

The best-known member of the lineage is **HERV-K(C4)**, which is integrated within the **complement C4 locus** of the human major histocompatibility complex (MHC). This insertion contributes to the well-known long and short C4 gene polymorphism and was the first HML10 provirus to be characterised. Subsequent genome-wide analyses identified additional HML10 loci, many of which share a striking preference for **intronic integration in the antisense orientation** relative to host genes.

Unlike several other HERV-K lineages, HML10 has attracted attention primarily because of its potential regulatory effects on host gene expression rather than the retention of viral coding capacity. Multiple HML10 loci possess transcriptionally active LTRs, and antisense transcription from proviral insertions has been proposed to influence the expression of neighbouring genes. In particular, an HML10 insertion within the *DAP3* gene has been shown experimentally to suppress DAP3 expression, suggesting that HML10-derived regulatory sequences can influence cellular pathways, including apoptosis. Similar regulatory mechanisms have been proposed for the HERV-K(C4) locus and other HML10 insertions, although their physiological significance remains under investigation.

Phylogenetic analyses have identified two principal HML10 subgroups, referred to as **Type I** and **Type II**, which differ primarily within portions of the *pol* and *env* genes. Despite these differences, all known human HML10 proviruses are extensively degraded and contain multiple defects that preclude the production of infectious retroviruses. Nevertheless, the lineage remains of considerable interest because of its unusual genomic organisation, its enrichment within host genes, and the growing evidence that HML10 loci have been co-opted as regulatory elements during primate evolution.
